# STE writing rules: short form

ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English (STE) is a controlled language for technical documentation. This file is a short form of Issue 9 (2025-01-15).
STE has two parts. Part 1 gives the writing rules (sections 1 thru 9). Part 2 is a controlled dictionary of approved words and not-approved words.
Each rule below has its number, a paraphrase in different words, and its page (`p. 46` = file `page_046.md` of the local copy of the standard).
This file is not the standard. If a paraphrase is not clear for your text, read the page.

## Limits for a checker

| Item | Maximum | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Sentence in a procedure (also each sentence of a warning or a caution) | 20 words | 5.1 |
| Sentence in a note | 25 words | 5.5 |
| Sentence in descriptive text | 25 words | 6.3 |
| Text before the colon of a vertical list | 20 words (procedure), 25 words (description) | 8.4 |
| Each item of a vertical list | 20 words (procedure), 25 words (description) | 8.4 |
| Sentences in one paragraph of descriptive text | 6 | 6.6 |
| Words in a multi-word noun (a hyphenated group is 1 word) | 3 | 2.1, 2.2 |
| Semicolons | 0 | 8.1 |

Section 8 gives the word count method. Count a sentence only after you apply rules 8.4 thru 8.7.

## Section 1: Words

### Which words you can use

- **1.1** (p. 46) STE permits three types of word only: approved words of the dictionary, technical nouns, and technical verbs. All other words are not permitted.
- **1.2** (p. 46) An approved word keeps the part of speech of its dictionary entry. Example: TEST is approved only as a noun. Write "Do a test of the backup procedure", not "Test the backup procedure". If the only alternative has a different part of speech, change the sentence construction (rule 9.1).
- **1.3** (p. 48) An approved word keeps the meaning that column 2 of its entry gives. Many approved words have a narrower meaning than in usual English. Example: FOLLOW (v) is only about a position or a sequence. To tell the reader to do what an instruction says, use OBEY.
- **1.4** (p. 48) For verbs and adjectives, only the forms printed in the entry are permitted. If an adjective has no listed comparative or superlative form, use MORE or MOST with the base form.

### Technical nouns

- **1.5** (p. 49) You can use a word as a technical noun if it goes into one or more of 22 categories. The dictionary does not list technical nouns. Keep them in a project glossary or a terminology database. The categories (pp. 49 thru 52 give example words):
  1. Official parts information
  2. Vehicles or machines, and locations on them
  3. Tools and support equipment, their parts, and locations on them
  4. Materials, consumables, and unwanted material
  5. Facilities, infrastructure, and logistic procedures
  6. Systems, components and circuits, their functions, configurations, and parts
  7. Mathematical, scientific, engineering terms, and formulas
  8. Navigation and geographic terms
  9. Numbers, units of measurement and time (and their symbols)
  10. Quoted text
  11. Professional roles, individuals, groups, organizations, and geopolitical entities
  12. Parts of the body
  13. Common personal effects, food, and beverages
  14. Medical terms
  15. Official documents, parts of documentation, standards, and guidelines
  16. Environmental and operational conditions
  17. Colors (no comparative or superlative forms, p. 52)
  18. Damage terms
  19. Computer science, information and communication technology
  20. Civil and military operations
  21. Law and regulations
  22. Animals, plants, and other life forms.
- **1.6** (p. 53) A lowercase (not approved) dictionary word is usable only when, in that context, it is a technical noun or a part of one. Example: "backup" is not approved as a general word, but it is a technical noun of category 19 in "The last backup is on the second disk". A word that is not in the dictionary is permitted only as a technical noun or a technical verb (flowchart, p. 144).
- **1.7** (p. 55) A technical noun stays a noun. Never make it a verb. Use an approved verb together with the noun. Example: not "Log the error", but "Write the error to the log". A word can be a technical noun and also a technical verb when it goes into a category of each type.
- **1.8** (p. 55) Prefer the terms that the official glossary of your organization, industry, or subject field gives.
- **1.9** (p. 56) If there is no approved term, select a short term (three words maximum) that readers understand easily. When an index number or a figure identifies the part, the base noun is sufficient.
- **1.10** (p. 56) A technical noun must be a term that all readers know. Regional terms, slang, and jargon are not permitted.
- **1.11** (p. 57) Use one technical noun for one item. Do not change the term in the text.

### Technical verbs

- **1.12** (p. 57) You can use a verb as a technical verb if it goes into one of four categories. Technical verbs obey all the rules of section 3. Pages 57 thru 58 give example verbs for each category:
  1. Manufacturing processes (to remove, add, or attach material, or to change the strength, the structure, the physical properties, the surface finish, or the shape of a material)
  2. Computer processes and applications (input and output, the user interface and applications, and system operations)
  3. Instructions and information for applicable subject fields (engineering and science, medical, civil and military operations, navigation, automotive and railway, and energy, oil, and gas)
  4. Law and regulations (only in legal texts, regulations, contracts, certificates, standards, and specifications).
- Limits for technical verbs (pp. 59 thru 60):
  - If an approved verb gives the same meaning, use the approved verb.
  - Use only a technical verb that is correct and clear in your context. Do not use a general one.
  - Do not make a technical verb from a technical noun when an approved verb and the noun can give the instruction.
  - A word that the dictionary marks as not approved can be a technical verb only in the context of its category.
- **1.13** (p. 60) A technical verb stays a verb. Never make it a noun. You can use the past participle of a technical verb as an adjective, for example "the encrypted file".

### Spelling

- **1.14** (p. 61) Spell in American English (the spelling of the dictionary). A contract, a specification, or a style guide can set a different spelling. Do not change the spelling of quoted text, for example text on a screen.

## Section 2: Multi-word nouns

- **2.1** (p. 63) A multi-word noun (a group of nouns and adjectives that has the function of one noun) has a maximum of three words. The head noun is usually the last word. To make a long group shorter, use prepositions (OF, ON, IN, FOR) or a relative clause. Example: not "database connection pool timeout value" (5 words), but "the timeout value of the database connection pool" (2 words and 3 words).
- **2.2** (p. 64) An official term of four or more words appears in full at its first use. After that, use one of two methods:
  - Method 1, shorter form (p. 64): in the remaining text, use a shorter form of three words or less, or an approved abbreviation. Do not make an abbreviation for a term of three words or less. Too many abbreviations make a procedure difficult to read (p. 65).
  - Method 2, hyphens (p. 66): put hyphens between the words that operate as one unit. A hyphenated group counts as one word. Connect only words that are related. Do not use hyphens to make one "word" from a group of more than three words. Do not add hyphens to a term of three words or less. Keep the hyphens of an official term.

## Section 3: Verbs

- **3.1** (p. 67) A verb form is permitted only if column 1 of the dictionary entry of that verb shows it.
- **3.2** (p. 68) These forms and tenses are permitted:
  - The infinitive
  - The imperative (command)
  - The simple present
  - The simple past
  - The simple future (WILL + verb)
  - The past participle, only as an adjective (rule 3.3).
- Other forms are not permitted, for example the present perfect, the past perfect, the progressive forms ("is running"), and all other complex constructions.
- **3.3** (p. 68) A past participle can only be an adjective: before a noun, or after a form of BE, BECOME, or STAY. It then shows a condition, not a passive action. Use only a past participle that the dictionary lists. Some approved adjectives look like the participle of a not-approved verb (for example, DAMAGED and PERMITTED). You can use them (p. 69).
- **3.4** (p. 69) Do not use auxiliary verbs with a past participle to make complex forms. This includes HAS, HAVE, or HAD + participle, and constructions such as "is to be", "can be", "must be", or "will be" + participle. Write the active form or the imperative.
- **3.5** (p. 70) "-ing" verb forms are not permitted. These are the exceptions (p. 71):
  - A technical noun, for example a heading such as "Troubleshooting"
  - A modifier in a technical noun that tells the function of an item, for example "logging level"
  - The small number of "-ing" words that the dictionary approves, for example DURING and REMAINING.
- **3.6** (p. 71) Write in the active voice. In procedures, do not use the passive voice. In descriptive text, the passive is acceptable only if nobody knows the agent. Test: in a passive sentence, the subject receives the action. The agent, if the sentence gives it, usually comes after BY (p. 72). Methods to make a sentence active (pp. 72 thru 74):
  - Make the agent the subject of the sentence.
  - Change an infinitive into the main verb.
  - In a procedure, use the imperative.
  - Use YOU (the reader) or WE (your organization) as the subject. SOMETHING can be the agent when the agent is unknown.
- **3.7** (p. 75) To tell about an action, use a verb, not a noun. Example: not "Before the installation of the agent, stop the service", but "Before you install the agent, stop the service". If the word is not an approved verb, use a different construction (for example, "do a check of").

## Section 4: Sentences

- **4.1** (p. 77) Keep each sentence short and clear. In a procedure, give each instruction directly in the imperative. In descriptive text, give one topic in each sentence and do not use the imperative (p. 78). In all text, be accurate. Tell the action or the condition, not an abstract statement.
- **4.2** (p. 79) A sentence keeps all its parts, also when this makes it longer. Keep the nouns, the verbs, the subject, and the articles. Do not use contractions ("don't", "isn't"). Write all words in full.
- **4.3** (p. 80) Use a vertical list for a long sentence with many items or actions. Format of a vertical list:
  - The text before the list ends with a colon.
  - A number, a letter, a dash, or a bullet identifies each item. Your style guide selects the mark.
  - Each item starts with an uppercase letter.
  - Where applicable, an article comes before the subject noun of each item.
  - An item that is a full sentence ends with a period. An item that is not a full sentence has no period (p. 82).
  - No item ends with a comma or a semicolon.
  - The last item ends with a period.
  - Each item connects correctly to the text before the colon (p. 82).
  - All items are at one level. Do not put a list in an item of a different list. Use parentheses for the sub-items (p. 82).
  - One list does not mix procedural items and descriptive items (p. 81).
  - In a safety instruction, each item has its own negative command (DO NOT) (p. 81).
- **4.4** (p. 83) Connect sentences that have related topics. Use connecting words (for example, AND, BUT, THEN, THUS) and connecting phrases (for example, "as a result", "at the same time"). THIS and THESE can also connect sentences.
- **4.5** (p. 84) A noun or a multi-word noun usually gets an article (THE, A, AN) or THIS or THESE in front of it. Details:
  - Do not use an article in a general statement or before an abstract concept.
  - In a long series of nouns, one article before the first noun is sufficient. Make sure that an adjective after that article applies only to the items that you want (p. 85).
  - Do not put a definite article before a noun that has an alphanumeric identifier after it (p. 85). Write "Stop server DB-02", not "Stop the server DB-02".

## Section 5: Procedural writing

A procedure gives instructions (work steps).

- **5.1** (p. 87) 20 words maximum in each sentence. Warnings, cautions, and other safety instructions also obey this limit. A note has a different limit: 25 words in each sentence (p. 88).
- **5.2** (p. 88) Give each sentence only one instruction. The exception is two or more actions that occur at the same time. Use numbers or letters to show the sequence of the steps. A step can have more than one sentence for simultaneous actions, or when a result or a limit comes immediately after the action (p. 89).
- **5.3** (p. 89) Every instruction uses the imperative (command form). Do not put MUST before the command. The exception is an instruction that is very important for safety, or an important condition (p. 90).
- **5.4** (p. 90) When the reader must know a condition first, start the sentence with the condition. Then put a comma, and then the command. Be careful: a comma in a different place can give a different meaning.
- **5.5** (p. 91) A note gives only information. A note is descriptive text and obeys section 6. Details:
  - Do not put an instruction, a requirement, or a limit in a note. Do not use the imperative in a note.
  - 25 words maximum in each sentence of a note.
  - Information that prevents injury or damage goes into a warning or a caution, not into a note (p. 92).
  - A limit, a tolerance, or a result goes into the work step, after the action (p. 92).
  - Test (p. 93): read the procedure without the notes. If the reader cannot do the procedure, move the missing information into a work step.
  - In descriptive text, use a note only for a figure or a table (p. 93).

## Section 6: Descriptive writing

Descriptive text gives information, not instructions (descriptions, reports, general information, notes). Do not use the imperative in it (p. 95).

- **6.1** (p. 95) Add information a little at a time, with one subject in each sentence. Do not give too much information too quickly.
- **6.2** (p. 96) Use key words and key phrases again in the subsequent sentences to connect the ideas. After you select a key word, do not change it. Connecting words and connecting phrases (rule 4.4) also help the reader.
- **6.3** (p. 98) 25 words maximum in each sentence.
- **6.4** (p. 99) Use paragraphs to keep related information together. Start each paragraph with a topic sentence.
- **6.5** (p. 100) Give only one topic in each paragraph. The topic sentence usually has a key word or a connecting word that connects it to the previous text.
- **6.6** (p. 101) Use a maximum of six sentences in a paragraph. If a paragraph has more, divide it. In the examples of the standard (pp. 96, 101), the text before a vertical list and the list together count as one sentence of the paragraph.

## Section 7: Safety instructions

Definitions (p. 103): warning = a risk of injury or death. Caution = a risk of damage to equipment or to other objects. Other industries can use other signal words (for example, DANGER or NOTICE) or symbols, but the content must obey rules 7.1 thru 7.3. STE gives no rules for the format. The uppercase letters of the examples are only a style (p. 104).

- **7.1** (p. 104) A signal word or a symbol tells the reader the level of risk. Do a risk analysis first. Then:
  - Risk of injury or death: warning
  - Risk of damage only: caution
  - The two risks together: warning.
- **7.2** (p. 105) The first words of a safety instruction are a precise command (for example, DO NOT ...) or a condition. If the reader must know a condition before the command, give the condition first.
- **7.3** (p. 106) Then tell the risk, or the possible result when the reader ignores the instruction.

Safety instructions also obey rule 5.1 (20 words in each sentence).

## Section 8: Punctuation and word count

### Punctuation

- **8.1** (p. 108) The semicolon is the only standard punctuation mark that STE does not permit. Write two sentences.
- **8.2** (p. 108) Put a hyphen only between words that have a direct relation. These are the usual cases:
  - A compound adjective before a noun, for example "a read-only volume"
  - A number or a fraction of two words, for example "twenty-four"
  - A letter or a number together with a noun, for example "a 2-port switch"
  - A compound verb with a first part that is not a verb, for example "hot-swap"
  - A prefix that ends with a vowel before a root that starts with a vowel, for example "re-enable".
- A hyphen is not a dash.
- **8.3** (p. 109) You can use parentheses for these items:
  - A reference to a figure or to text
  - The number or the letter of an item
  - The number of a work step
  - An abbreviation
  - The singular and the plural at the same time, for example "file(s)"
  - A short explanation of a word or of a part of the sentence (p. 110)
  - An alternative (p. 110).

### Word count

- **8.4** (p. 110) For the word count, the colon of a vertical list ends a sentence, as a period does:
  - The text before the colon is one sentence. Its limit is 20 words in a procedure and 25 words in descriptive text.
  - Each item after the colon is a new sentence, with the same limit.
- **8.5** (p. 111) A parenthesis, with all its content, is one word of the sentence around it. Its content is also a different sentence, and you count it separately. An identifier or an abbreviation in parentheses also counts as one word.
- **8.6** (p. 111) Each of these items counts as one word:
  - A number, also a written number with a hyphen (p. 111)
  - A number with its unit of measurement, with the unit as a symbol or in full words (p. 112)
  - An abbreviation, an acronym, or an initialism, also a time such as "10 a.m." together with its number (p. 112)
  - An alphanumeric identifier, for example "No. 3" or "SRV-12" (p. 112)
  - Quoted text: text in quotation marks, text that uppercase letters or a different font identify (for example, a label on a screen, or `code` in a monospace font), and a formula (p. 113)
  - A title, a heading, and the text on a placard or on a label (p. 113)
  - The proper noun of a person, a group, an organization, or a geopolitical entity (p. 114).
- Do not count a number that identifies a paragraph or a work step (p. 112).
- **8.7** (p. 114) Words that hyphens connect are one word for the count.

## Section 9: Writing practices

- **9.1** (p. 115) If a direct replacement of one word does not give a good sentence, build the sentence in a different way. These conditions make a different construction necessary (pp. 116 thru 117):
  - The alternative has a different part of speech, and the grammar of the sentence must change.
  - The word-for-word result has no clear meaning.
  - The alternative changes the meaning of the sentence.
  - The word is not in the dictionary.
- After a change, read the remaining text again and make sure that it is still correct (p. 118).
- **9.2** (p. 119) Every approved word keeps its approved meaning (read column 2) and its approved part of speech (p. 120). Usually, an approved word has only one approved meaning.
- **9.3** (p. 121) Do not join a verb and a particle into a phrasal verb (a verb and a preposition or an adverb that together have a new meaning, for example "set up" or "carry out"). The dictionary usually does not list phrasal verbs as not approved, thus you must find them yourself (p. 122). The dictionary approves a few phrasal verbs, each with a restricted meaning. The list of approved verbs (p. 147) shows them.
- **9.4** (p. 122) Keep terms and wording the same in all the text. Use the same term for the same item. Use the same wording each time the same type of step or statement occurs.

## General recommendations GR-1 thru GR-8

The general recommendations are not rules. They help you to prevent frequent errors (p. 123).

- **GR-1** (p. 123) Write the conjunction THAT after verbs such as MAKE SURE, SHOW, and RECOMMEND. It shows where the subordinate clause starts, and it helps translation.
- **GR-2** (p. 124) WITH is approved with three meanings (an association, help, or a means). Read each sentence with WITH again and remove the ambiguity. When you write the sentence again without WITH, keep the primary action verb. Write "Stop the job with the CLI", not "Use the CLI to stop the job".
- **GR-3** (p. 125) Use only the pronouns of the dictionary. Do not use "he" or "she". If a pronoun can refer to more than one noun, write the noun again.
- **GR-4** (p. 125) The reader must know which item the pronoun THIS refers to. If two items are possible, give the context again.
- **GR-5** (p. 126) False friends: if English is not your first language, make sure that a word has the meaning that you want in English. A similar word in your language can have a different meaning.
- **GR-6** (p. 126) Do not use Latin abbreviations ("e.g.", "i.e.", "etc."). Use English words (for example, "for example"), or remove them.
- **GR-7** (p. 127) Use gender-neutral language. Do not use "he" or "she". Use "man" or "woman" only when the context makes it necessary, for example in a medical text.
- **GR-8** (p. 127) The possessive form ('s) is permitted. If you have a doubt about it in a sentence, write the sentence without it.

## The dictionary

The dictionary (part 2) has 875 approved words and 1274 not-approved words (numbers from p. 131). It does not list technical nouns or technical verbs as entries. It uses American English spelling as in Merriam-Webster.

| Column | Content |
|---|---|
| 1. Word (part of speech) | The entry word in bold. UPPERCASE = approved. Lowercase = not approved (p. 132). |
| 2. Approved meaning / ALTERNATIVES | For an approved word: its approved meaning. The definition text itself is not STE (p. 137). For a not-approved word: the approved alternatives in UPPERCASE, each with its part of speech. (TN) marks a technical noun and (TV) marks a technical verb. An alternative can also be a phrase without a part of speech (p. 138). |
| 3. STE EXAMPLE | One possible STE sentence. It is a recommendation, not the only correct form (p. 142). Many examples come from aircraft maintenance. |
| 4. Non-STE example | How the not-approved word occurs in usual English. Empty for an approved word, unless a help text gives restricted meanings (p. 143). |

How to read an entry:

- **Parts of speech** (pp. 132 thru 133): n, v, adj, adv, pron, art, prep, conj.
- **Nouns** (p. 133): the dictionary gives only the singular. You can use the plural of a countable approved noun, unless a help text tells you differently.
- **Verbs** (pp. 133 thru 135): column 1 gives all the permitted forms (base, -s form, past, past participle). A form that is not in column 1 is not permitted. Some auxiliary and modal verbs have fewer forms, for example BE (only BE, IS, ARE, WAS, WERE) and WILL (only WILL). A verb with an object can have a different meaning from the same verb without an object.
- **Adjectives** (p. 136): the base form, with the comparative and the superlative in parentheses when they exist. Otherwise use MORE or MOST.
- **Adverbs** (p. 136): each adverb is an entry of its own. Make the comparative with MORE or MOST.
- **Alternatives** (pp. 137 thru 138): they are only suggestions. The first alternative usually has the same part of speech. If you select an alternative with a different part of speech, change the sentence construction (rule 9.1).
- **Help texts** (pp. 139 thru 141) have four categories:
  1. A recommendation about the correct use of the word
  2. A restricted meaning, with the alternatives for other meanings
  3. A word that is approved for one context only (for example, safety instructions)
  4. Other important information, also for not-approved words.
- **Opposites** (p. 131): an entry does not include its antonym. An approved word does not make its opposite approved. Look up each word.
- **Look up the base form**: the singular noun, the infinitive of the verb, the base form of the adjective. An inflected form is permitted only if its base entry is approved and, for a verb, if column 1 lists that form.

Word selection (flowchart, p. 144):

1. If the word is in the dictionary and approved, make sure that the part of speech and the meaning are correct. If they are, use the word.
2. If the word is not approved, read its alternatives and their examples. Select an alternative. If it has the same part of speech, replace the word. If not, write a different sentence construction.
3. If the word is not in the dictionary (or its meaning is not approved), use it only as a technical noun or a technical verb of a category. Add it to the project glossary with its category. If it is not in a category, do not use it.

Other lists: the list of recurring errors is on pp. 145 thru 146. The list of approved verbs is on p. 147.
